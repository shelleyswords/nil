# Chapter 1 — Don't Fumble the First Check

### The mindset that protects you

Getting your first NIL check feels like winning. The danger is treating it like a prize instead of a tool. Here's the truth nobody tells you:

- **NIL money is not "extra."** It's income. The IRS sees it exactly like a paycheck.
- **It probably won't last forever.** Most NIL deals are short. The money you make now might need to stretch for years.
- **The goal isn't to look rich. It's to *be* rich later.** Looking rich (car, jewelry, bottle service) is how you go broke. Being rich is quiet.

### Why pro athletes go broke

You've heard the stat: a huge share of pro athletes are broke within a few years of retiring. It's almost never because they didn't make enough. It's because:

1. They spent like the money would never stop.
2. They trusted the wrong people with it.
3. They paid enormous fees and never noticed.
4. Nobody ever taught them the basics.

This guide is the antidote to all four. You're reading it, which already puts you ahead.

### The 3 mistakes that do the most damage

- **Spending it before taxing it.** That $20k deal is not $20k. Roughly a quarter to a third belongs to the government. Spend the whole thing and you'll owe money you no longer have.
- **Handing it to an "advisor" who charges a fortune.** More on this in Chapter 3 — it's the silent killer.
- **Lifestyle creep.** Every dollar you spend "leveling up" your lifestyle is a dollar that can't grow. The car loses value; the index fund grows.

> **✅ Do This Now**
> - Decide *before* the money hits: "This is a tool, not a trophy."
> - Make a rule: **no big purchases for 30 days** after any new deal. Let the excitement cool.
> - Read the next chapter before you spend a dollar of it.

# Chapter 2 — Taxes First (The Part Nobody Warns You About)

This is the chapter that saves you from a nightmare. NIL income is **taxable, and nobody withholds it for you.** Unlike a normal job, the money arrives whole — and the IRS expects its share later. If you spend all of it, tax time becomes a disaster.

### What you actually owe

When you earn NIL money, you're basically self-employed. That means you may owe:

- **Federal income tax** (the rate depends on your total income)
- **State income tax** (depends on your state — some have none)
- **Self-employment tax (~15.3%)** — this is the big surprise. It covers Social Security and Medicare, which a normal employer would split with you. As a self-employed athlete, you cover it all.

**Rule of thumb: set aside 25–35% of every NIL dollar for taxes the moment it arrives.** When in doubt, use 30%. Put it in a separate savings account and pretend it doesn't exist.

### Quarterly payments (don't get caught off guard)

Because nobody withholds taxes for you, the IRS wants you to pay **estimated taxes four times a year**, not just in April. Skip them and you can owe penalties on top of the tax. The dates are roughly April, June, September, and January.

### Deductions can lower the bill

Because you're self-employed, legitimate business expenses can reduce what you owe — things like equipment, travel for deals, a portion of your phone, agent/marketing costs. **Keep every receipt.** A simple folder or app is enough.

### The 1099 form

Companies that pay you $600+ will usually send you a **1099 form** at year's end reporting what they paid you. The IRS gets a copy too — so the income is on record whether you report it or not. Always report it.

> **✅ Do This Now**
> - Open a **separate "Taxes" savings account.** The day any NIL money lands, move **30%** into it. Don't touch it.
> - Start a **receipts folder** (physical or an app) for every business expense.
> - Put the **quarterly tax dates** in your phone calendar now.
> - Before your first tax season with real NIL income, **spend ~$200–400 on one session with a CPA** (a tax pro). It will save you far more than it costs. This is the *one* professional worth paying early.

# Chapter 3 — The Fee Trap (The Silent Killer)

This is the chapter that puts the most money back in your pocket. Fees are invisible — they don't send you a bill, they just quietly skim your account — which is exactly why they're so dangerous.

### Why a "small" 1% fee is enormous

An advisor who charges **1% per year** sounds cheap. It isn't. Here's why:

- That 1% comes off your **entire balance, every year, forever** — whether they make you money or not.
- Worse, it's 1% of money that *would have grown.* Every dollar taken in fees is a dollar that never compounds.

**The rough math:** Invest steadily starting in your early 20s and a 1% annual fee can cost you **$300,000–$500,000+** by retirement compared to a near-free index fund. Same investments. The *only* difference is the fee. That's a house. Gone, to fees.

### The three ways people pay too much

1. **Percentage-of-assets advisors (1%+/year)** — the most common trap. Fine for the ultra-wealthy with complex needs; overkill and overpriced for a young athlete with a simple situation.
2. **Commission salespeople** — they get paid to sell you specific products (like certain insurance or annuities). The product that pays *them* the most is rarely the one that's best for *you.* Be very skeptical of anyone selling "whole life insurance" or "annuities" to a healthy 20-year-old.
3. **High-fee funds (expense ratios).** Even without an advisor, some investment funds charge 1%+ internally. A good index fund charges around **0.03%–0.10%.** Always check the "expense ratio" before buying any fund.

### What good actually costs

- **DIY index investing:** nearly free (fund fees around 0.03–0.10%).
- **A fee-only, flat-rate financial planner:** you pay a set fee (e.g. a few hundred dollars) for a one-time plan or hourly advice. **This is the honest model.** They don't take a cut of your money.
- **Avoid:** anyone who wants an ongoing % of everything you have, unless you have genuinely complex, large-scale needs.

> **The key phrase to learn: "fee-only fiduciary."** A *fiduciary* is legally required to act in your best interest. *Fee-only* means they don't earn commissions from selling you things. Ask any advisor point-blank: *"Are you a fee-only fiduciary, and how exactly do you get paid?"* If the answer is fuzzy, walk away.

> **✅ Do This Now**
> - Before paying *anyone* to manage your money, ask: **"How do you get paid?"** Make them say a number.
> - Never buy a fund without checking its **expense ratio.** Aim for under 0.20%; great ones are under 0.10%.
> - Be deeply skeptical of anyone selling **whole life insurance or annuities** to a young, healthy athlete.

# Chapter 4 — The Simple Foundation (Before You Invest a Dollar)

Investing is exciting, but if you skip the foundation, one emergency can wipe it out. Do these in order.

### Step 1 — A starter emergency fund

Life happens — a flight home, a busted phone, a medical bill. Without a cushion, you end up selling investments at the worst time or going into debt. **Start with $1,000 set aside.** Build toward **3–6 months of your basic expenses** over time. Keep it boring and reachable.

### Step 2 — Park it in a high-yield savings account (HYSA)

A normal big-bank savings account pays almost nothing. A **high-yield savings account** (offered by many online banks) pays meaningfully more interest for the exact same safety, with the same federal (FDIC) insurance. There's no reason to keep your emergency fund or tax money in an account paying 0%.

- Use it for: emergency fund, tax set-aside, short-term goals.
- It is **not** investing — it's safe storage that earns a little. That's the point.

### Step 3 — Kill high-interest debt

If you have **credit card debt** (often 20%+ interest), paying it off is the best "investment" you can make — no investment reliably beats a guaranteed 20% return. Knock it out before you put money in the market. (Low-interest debt like some student loans is less urgent — that's a Chapter 6 conversation.)

> **✅ Do This Now**
> - Open a **high-yield savings account** (5 minutes online).
> - Move your **$1,000 starter emergency fund** there. This is separate from your tax account.
> - List any **debts and their interest rates.** Anything above ~8–10%, attack it now.

# Chapter 5 — Investing Without Getting Ripped Off

Now the fun part — making your money grow. The secret: **it's supposed to be boring.** Boring wins. Exciting is how people lose money.

### The big idea: own a little of everything, cheaply, for a long time

You don't need to pick winning stocks. You don't need to watch the market. You need to own a tiny slice of *thousands* of companies and leave it alone for years. The tool that does this is an **index fund.**

### What's an index fund?

Instead of betting on one company (risky — it can crash), an **index fund** buys a tiny piece of *all* the big companies at once. When the overall economy grows over time, so does your money. It's automatically diversified, and it's nearly free to own.

- A fund tracking the **"total stock market"** or the **"S&P 500"** (the 500 biggest U.S. companies) is the classic core holding.
- Historically, the broad market has trended up over long periods — through ups and downs. The key word is **long.**
- **This beats most professional stock-pickers.** That's not an opinion — most expensive, actively-managed funds fail to beat a simple index over time. You can win by being boring and cheap.

### The account matters as much as the fund: open a Roth IRA

*Where* you hold investments changes how much tax you pay on the growth. The best starter account for most young people is a **Roth IRA:**

- You put in money you've **already paid tax on**, and then **all the growth is tax-free forever.** Decades of gains, no tax on the way out.
- Because you're young, that tax-free compounding is worth an enormous amount.
- There's an annual contribution limit (a few thousand dollars per year) and an income limit, but most college athletes fit comfortably.
- A Roth IRA is **not** an investment itself — it's a *bucket* you open at a brokerage, then buy index funds *inside* it.

### The "boring portfolio" that quietly wins

A simple, widely-taught starting framework (remember — general education, not a recommendation for you specifically):

1. Open a **Roth IRA** at a low-cost brokerage (the big well-known ones charge $0 to open and trade).
2. Inside it, buy a **broad, low-cost index fund** (total market or S&P 500, expense ratio under 0.10%).
3. **Add money regularly** — automatically, every month, no matter what the market is doing.
4. **Don't touch it.** Don't panic-sell when it dips. Dips are normal. Time in the market beats timing the market.

That's it. That simple system, done for years, builds real wealth.

### What to avoid

- **Meme stocks, crypto gambling, day-trading, "hot tips."** These are entertainment, not investing. If you ever play with them, use only money you can afford to lose — never your foundation.
- **Anything promising guaranteed high returns.** That's the #1 sign of a scam (Chapter 6).
- **Complicated products you don't understand.** If you can't explain it simply, don't buy it.

> **✅ Do This Now**
> - Open a **Roth IRA** at a major low-cost brokerage.
> - Set up an **automatic monthly contribution**, even if it's small to start.
> - Inside the Roth, buy a **broad low-cost index fund.** Then leave it alone.
> - Promise yourself: **no panic-selling on a dip. Ever.**

# Chapter 6 — Protecting Yourself (Scams, "Friends," and the Fine Print)

Money attracts people. The more visible you are, the more they come. This chapter keeps your money — and your name — safe.

### Spotting scams and bad actors

Red flags that should make you run:

- **"Guaranteed" returns.** No legitimate investment guarantees high returns. None.
- **Pressure to act *right now.*** Urgency is a manipulation tactic. Real opportunities wait a day.
- **You don't understand it.** Confusion is a sales tool. If they can't explain it plainly, that's on purpose.
- **They found you in your DMs.** Be extremely skeptical of unsolicited "investment opportunities," crypto coaches, and "I help athletes" cold messages.

### The "friends and family with a deal" problem

Some of the hardest money situations aren't strangers — they're people you love. A cousin's business that needs investment. A buddy's "can't-miss" startup. A family member who needs a loan.

- It's okay to help people. It's **not** okay to risk your future on it.
- A simple rule: **only "invest" money you'd be fine never seeing again,** and never your foundation (tax money, emergency fund, retirement).
- "No" is a complete sentence. You can even blame your plan: *"My money's all locked into a long-term setup, I can't touch it."*

### Read before you sign

NIL deals are contracts. Watch for:

- **Exclusivity** (does this deal stop you from working with competitors?)
- **Length** (how long are you locked in?)
- **What they can use** (your name, image, for how long, where?)
- **What you must deliver** (how many posts, appearances?)

If a deal is more than pocket change, **have someone who knows contracts look at it.** A short paid review beats a bad multi-year commitment.

### Should you form an LLC?

As your NIL income grows, some athletes set up a **business entity (like an LLC)** to run their NIL income through. It can offer tax flexibility and liability protection. It's **not** necessary for your first small deals, but it's a smart question to raise with a **CPA** once you're earning consistently. Don't overcomplicate it early.

### When you actually DO need a pro (and how to hire one without overpaying)

You don't need a full-time money manager. You *might* want:

- A **CPA** for taxes (worth it as soon as you have real income).
- A **fee-only, flat-rate financial planner** for a one-time plan when your situation gets complex.
- A **lawyer** to review significant contracts.

**How to hire without getting fleeced:**
- Ask: **"Are you a fee-only fiduciary? How do you get paid? What's the total cost in dollars?"**
- Prefer **flat fees or hourly** over "percentage of my money."
- Get it in writing. Walk away from anyone vague about how they're paid.

> **✅ Do This Now**
> - Adopt the rule: **no unsolicited DM investment is ever real.** Delete and move on.
> - Decide your **"money I'd never miss" limit** for helping friends/family — and never cross it.
> - For any meaningful contract, **get a paid review** before signing.
> - Save the magic question in your notes: *"Are you a fee-only fiduciary, and how exactly do you get paid?"*

# Chapter 7 — Your Year-One Plan (Step by Step)

Everything in this guide, turned into one ordered checklist. Do them in order. By income level at the end.

### The universal order of operations

1. **Set aside 30% for taxes** the day money arrives (separate account). → Ch. 2
2. **Build a $1,000 starter emergency fund** in a high-yield savings account. → Ch. 4
3. **Pay off high-interest (credit card) debt.** → Ch. 4
4. **Grow the emergency fund** toward 3–6 months of expenses. → Ch. 4
5. **Open a Roth IRA** and start automatic monthly investing into a low-cost index fund. → Ch. 5
6. **Keep learning, keep it boring, don't touch the investments.** → Ch. 5
7. **Protect yourself** — scam rules, contract reviews, the magic question. → Ch. 6

### Tailored by income level

**If you're earning ~$1k–$10k/year:**
- Taxes set-aside is still #1 (even small amounts).
- Build the $1,000 emergency fund.
- Open the Roth IRA and invest even $25–50/month — the *habit* matters more than the amount right now.
- Don't pay anyone a % to manage this. It's too simple to need it.

**If you're earning ~$10k–$50k/year:**
- All of the above, but bigger set-asides and a full 3–6 month emergency fund.
- **See a CPA** before your first tax season — non-negotiable at this level.
- Max out Roth IRA contributions if you can.
- Start a receipts/expenses system for deductions.
- Consider asking the CPA whether an LLC makes sense yet.

**If you're earning $50k+/year:**
- Everything above, plus:
- A **CPA is mandatory**, and quarterly estimated payments matter a lot.
- Consider a **one-time, flat-fee financial planner** (fee-only fiduciary) for a personalized plan — paid by the hour or flat, *never* a % of assets.
- Get **every significant contract** reviewed by a lawyer.
- Stay disciplined: the bigger the money, the louder the lifestyle temptation and the more people show up. Your boring plan is your edge.

# Chapter 8 — Resources & Quick Reference

### The 10-second gut checks

- **Before spending a new check:** "Did I move 30% to taxes? Have I waited 30 days?"
- **Before buying any investment:** "What's the expense ratio? Do I understand it?"
- **Before hiring anyone:** "Are they a fee-only fiduciary? How do they get paid, in dollars?"
- **Before any 'opportunity':** "Is it guaranteed or urgent? Then it's probably a scam."

### The magic questions to ask any advisor

1. "Are you a **fiduciary**, legally required to act in my best interest — in writing?"
2. "Are you **fee-only**, or do you earn commissions from products you sell me?"
3. "What is the **total cost to me, in dollars**, per year?"
4. "Can you explain your recommendation in plain English?"

### Glossary (plain English)

- **Index fund** — a single investment that owns a tiny piece of hundreds or thousands of companies at once. Cheap, diversified, boring, effective.
- **Expense ratio** — the yearly fee a fund charges, as a %. Lower is better. Under 0.10% is great.
- **Roth IRA** — a retirement account you fund with already-taxed money; all growth comes out tax-free later. Great for young earners.
- **High-yield savings account (HYSA)** — a safe savings account that pays real interest. For emergency funds and tax money.
- **Fiduciary** — someone legally required to act in your best interest.
- **Fee-only** — an advisor paid only by you (flat/hourly), not by commissions. The honest model.
- **1099** — the tax form reporting money a company paid you. The IRS gets a copy.
- **Estimated/quarterly taxes** — tax payments you make 4x/year because no employer withholds for you.
- **Self-employment tax** — the ~15.3% that covers Social Security/Medicare when you work for yourself.
- **Compounding** — your money earning money, then *that* earning money. The reason starting young is a superpower.

### Tools worth knowing (categories, not endorsements)

- A **high-yield savings account** from a reputable online bank.
- A **major low-cost brokerage** for your Roth IRA and index funds.
- A simple **expense/receipt tracking app** for tax deductions.
- A **fee-only fiduciary directory** to find honest, flat-fee planners when you need one.

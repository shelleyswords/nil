# L12 — The fee trap: how 1% steals a fortune

**Lesson ID:** L12
**Module:** 4 — Investing Without Getting Ripped Off

**Learning objective:** Understand how a seemingly small 1% annual fee compounds into a $300k–$500k+ loss over a lifetime, and the three ways people overpay.

**Action step(s):** Commit to always asking "How do you get paid?" before letting anyone manage your money, and to checking the expense ratio before buying any fund.

**Runtime estimate:** ~5 min

---

## Voiceover script

This is the lesson that, all by itself, can be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars to you. [pause] I'm not being dramatic. Stick with me, because once you understand fees, you'll never get quietly robbed the way most people do.

Here's what makes fees so dangerous: **they're invisible.** [pause] A fee doesn't send you a bill. It doesn't ask permission. It just quietly skims a slice off your account, over and over, while you're not looking. No alert, no receipt. That silence is *exactly* why fees do so much damage — you never feel the cut.

Let's talk about the classic one: an advisor who charges **one percent a year.** [pause] One percent. Sounds tiny, right? Almost rude to even worry about. Here's why it's anything but.

That one percent comes off your **entire balance, every single year, forever.** [pause] Not one percent of your profits. One percent of *everything you own* — whether they made you money that year or *lost* you money. Win or lose, they take their slice.

But here's the part that actually hurts. [pause] It's not just the dollar they take. It's that the dollar they took *would have grown.* Every dollar skimmed in fees is a dollar that can never compound into more. You don't just lose the fee — you lose everything that fee *would have become* over the decades. And that's where it gets ugly.

Let me give you the number. [pause] Imagine two athletes. Same money, invested steadily starting in their early twenties. Same exact investments. The *only* difference: one pays a one-percent annual fee, the other uses a nearly-free index fund. By retirement, that one-percent fee can cost the first athlete somewhere between **three hundred thousand and five hundred thousand dollars. Or more.** [pause] Same investments. Same effort. The *only* difference is the fee. That's a *house.* Handed over, quietly, to fees — for nothing.

So let me show you the **three ways people overpay**, so you can spot every one. [pause]

**One: percentage-of-assets advisors.** The one-percent-a-year crowd. This is the most common trap. It's *fine* for the ultra-wealthy with genuinely complicated lives. For a young athlete with a simple setup? It's overkill and overpriced.

**Two: commission salespeople.** [pause] These folks get paid to *sell* you specific products — certain kinds of insurance, things called annuities. The problem: the product that pays *them* the biggest commission is almost never the one that's best for *you.* Be very, very skeptical of anyone pushing "whole life insurance" or "annuities" on a healthy twenty-year-old. That's a giant red flag.

**Three: high-fee funds.** [pause] Even with no advisor at all, some investment funds quietly charge over one percent *internally* — it's called the **expense ratio**, the fund's own yearly fee. A *good* index fund? It charges almost nothing — like point-zero-three to point-one-zero percent. Always, always check that expense ratio before you buy any fund. We'll go deep on that next lesson.

So here are your two permanent rules. [pause] **One:** before you let *anyone* manage your money, ask point-blank — *"How do you get paid?"* — and make them say an actual number. **Two:** never buy a fund without checking its expense ratio first.

Those two habits alone put you ahead of people earning ten times what you earn. Fees are the silent killer — and you just learned to hear them coming.
